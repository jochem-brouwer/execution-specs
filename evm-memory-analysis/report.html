What can we do about EVM memory?

The quadratic memory price is harsh — but the fix must not hand clients unbounded memory, and must not break existing contracts. A look at today's worst case, and at two ways to change the cost model.

00

The short version

Two knobs are being conflated. Price (how much gas a byte of memory costs) and the ceiling (how much memory can exist at once) are independent, and the cleanest designs set them separately.

Recommendation

Keep a cheap, linear price (kills the harsh quadratic) but bound the aggregate the way EIP-7686 does: make each frame reserve gas equal to the memory it holds before forwarding to a child. That turns the transaction gas budget into a composable, non-griefable memory ceiling — N gas ⇒ ≤ N bytes — with a tunable multiplier to pin the ceiling at or below today's level. Price and ceiling stay decoupled.

A bare price cut (EIP-7923 without a working cap) makes a single ordinary transaction allocate ~654 MiB. A transaction-global cap fixes the size but breaks composability (griefing). A per-frame size cap doesn't bound the aggregate at all.

01

How EVM memory is priced today

Memory is charged by how far it has been expanded, in 32-byte words, with a linear term and a quadratic term:

memory_cost(w) = 3·w + w² / 512 // w = 32-byte words

The quadratic term is what makes large single-frame memory expensive: at the ceiling the marginal cost is hundreds of gas per word. Under the EIP-7825 transaction gas cap of 16,777,216 gas, that puts a hard, self-imposed wall on how much one frame can allocate — 2.80 MiB — without any explicit memory limit in the protocol. The quadratic is the current limiter.

02

Today's worst case, measured

Three different quantities matter, and an adversary maximizes each differently. We reproduced all three as fill-able benchmarks.

Single frame
2.80 MiB

One MSTORE at offset 2,939,456 (91,859 words). Costs 16,756,193 gas — 14 gas under the cap.

Total — peak live
18.3 MiB

~148 nested self-calls. Each fresh frame resets memory to zero, escaping the quadratic. Bounded by the 63/64 forwarding rule, not the 1024 depth limit.

Cumulative touched
~126 MiB

~14,800 sequential calls that expand then return; unused gas comes back, so 63/64 isn't a loss. Only ~8.7 KiB is ever live.

Why "total" ≠ "single". The quadratic punishes concentration, so an attacker splits the allocation across nested frames — each frame pays the quadratic from a zero base. The gain tops out at ~6.5× because deeper frames get exponentially less gas (each call can forward at most 63/64 of what remains).

Why "cumulative" ≠ "total". Peak live memory is what a client must hold in RAM; cumulative touched is expansion/zeroing work. Freeing and re-touching (sequential calls, gas returned) lets a transaction do far more expansion work than it ever holds at once.

03

The case against the quadratic

The quadratic term is a blunt instrument. It makes memory cheap for small buffers and punishingly expensive for large ones — which is good for limiting size, but bad for anyone who legitimately needs a large contiguous buffer (proof verification, large returndata, ZK-friendly execution). It also creates a non-obvious cost cliff and, as shown above, incentivizes the nesting workaround that partially defeats it anyway.

EIP-7923 is pointed in the right direction: drop the quadratic, price memory linearly. The open question is what then stops a transaction from allocating an unreasonable amount — because a cheap linear price removes the only thing currently bounding memory.

04

Option A — EIP-7923: page-based linear price

Charge a flat 100 gas per 4096-byte page (page 0 free per frame); both linear and quadratic word terms removed. A transaction-global page counter enforces a MAXIMUM_MEMORY_SIZE of 64 MiB.

ALLOCATE_PAGE_COST = 100 PAGE_SIZE = 4096 // ≈ 0.78 gas/word MAXIMUM_MEMORY_SIZE = 64 MiB = 16,384 pages // transaction-global

Effect on the maximum

The price is ~4× cheaper than even today's linear floor, and there is no quadratic. That inverts the whole picture:

StrategyToday (quadratic)EIP-7923, cap OFFNote
Single frame2.8 MiB~654 MiBCap-free linear price; one MSTORE.
Total (nested)18.3 MiB~654 MiBNesting no longer helps — no quadratic to escape, 63/64 becomes pure loss.
Cumulative126 MiB~654 MiBChurn no longer helps — call overhead ≥ a page's price.

All three collapse to ~654 MiB = gas / 100 × 4096. Removing the quadratic removes the very reason the nesting and churn tricks existed, so the naive single-frame path becomes the worst case — and it is 234× larger than today's single frame. This is exactly the DoS vector the 64 MiB cap exists to close.

The cap is the hard part

Transaction-global cap

Bounds the total, but the budget is shared across frames: a parent that uses most of it leaves a callee unable to allocate — a griefing vector that breaks the EVM's promise that each call has independent memory. Also fails the transaction the instant it's hit.

Per-frame size cap

Composable, but doesn't bound the aggregate. At 100 gas/page, a 2.8 MiB per-frame cap still yields 275 MiB via ~99 nested frames. Holding the total at today's ~18 MiB would need a ~56 KiB per-frame cap — far too small for real contracts.

The scope of the cap isn't the lever — price is. The worst-case total is gas / price-per-byte; 654 MiB happens only because 100 gas/page is ~35× too cheap to let the gas cap bind at today's level. Making gas bind at ~18 MiB via price alone would mean ~3,570 gas/page (~28 gas/word) — which overcharges small, common buffers ~9×. Price alone can't be both cheap-for-small and bounded-for-large.

05

Option B — EIP-7686: linear price + memory-aware forwarding

EIP-7686 keeps a linear price but adds the missing piece: it bounds the aggregate through a gas reservation in the call-forwarding rule, not through a price or a counter.

memory_cost(w) = 3·w // linear; quadratic removed max_call_gas(gas, mem_bytes) = gas − max(gas/64, mem_bytes) // forwarding // + a per-call cap: memory may not exceed the call's initial gas

Why this is the elegant bound

Before forwarding to a child, a frame must reserve gas equal to the bytes it currently holds. So every ancestor of the deepest frame has locked away gas equal to its own live memory, and:

Σ(all live memory) ≤ (gas reserved by ancestors) + (deepest frame ≤ its gas) ≤ total gas

That is a transaction-wide invariant — N gas ⇒ ≤ N bytes — produced by a purely per-frame rule. No global counter, so no griefing: a callee's memory limit is simply its own forwarded gas, exactly like gas itself. It also generalizes the 63/64 rule — small memory falls back to plain 63/64; large memory reserves the bytes.

And it stays cheap: the bound comes from reserving gas 1:1 with bytes, not paying it. Filling the entire ceiling costs only ~1.57M gas (~9% of the budget) — the rest is merely held back.

This is the decoupling we were missing: the reservation multiplier sets the ceiling independently of the price. Keep memory cheap and linear, and pick k for whatever aggregate ceiling policy wants.

06

The knob is gas-per-byte

Take the plainest step first: keep only today's linear term — 3 gas / 32-byte word — drop the quadratic, with no cap and no reservation. A single frame can then allocate ~170 MiB, and because there is no quadratic left to escape, nesting and churn stop helping — single, total and cumulative all land on the same 170 MiB.

That exposes the general law. Once the quadratic is gone, the maximum memory is simply:

max memory = GAS ÷ (gas per byte)

Every proposal is one choice of that single number. The quadratic's real job was to make gas-per-byte rise with size — self-limiting; a flat rate cannot, so a flat rate buys a flat ceiling.

Why EIP-7686 is "off by 32/3" — and why that is the whole point.

EIP-7686 charges the cheap linear rate (3/32 gas/byte) but reserves 1 gas/byte on forwarding — exactly 32/3 ≈ 10.7× the linear cost. The reservation, not the payment, sets the ceiling: the same linear price that alone yields 170 MiB collapses to a 16 MiB N gas ⇒ N byte bound. It decouples what you pay from what bounds you — cheap to use, tight ceiling. Pure linear pricing ties the two together, so it can be cheap or bounded, never both.

08

What breaks — compatibility

EIP-7923 · price

Making memory cheaper breaks nothing by cost — existing code only ever pays less. The risk is entirely in the cap:

  • A 64 MiB cap breaks no contract today (max is 18.3 MiB) but permits ~3.5× today's client RAM.
  • Any cap below ~18 MiB can break existing nested-memory patterns.
  • A global cap introduces a new failure mode: a callee OOMs based on caller behaviour (griefing) — not observable in any pre-fork trace.

EIP-7686 · forwarding

The reservation changes an observable quantity: gas forwardable to a child drops when the parent holds memory.

  • A contract that builds a large buffer (e.g. copies big calldata/returndata) then calls out will forward gas − mem_bytes instead of 63/64·gas — the child can OOG where it previously succeeded.
  • The per-call cap (memory ≤ initial gas) is generous at k=1 and rarely binds; it tightens as k grows.
  • Action: measure how many mainnet call sites forward near-all gas while holding > a few KiB of memory — that set is the blast radius.
09

Recommendation

You cannot have all three of {cheap small usage, linear/predictable, bounded aggregate} from a price alone — pick the design that adds a separate bound. The reservation model does this best.

Adopt EIP-7686's reservation model; tune the ceiling with k

  • Price: linear (drop the quadratic) — cheap, predictable, ZK-friendly. This is the EIP-7923 direction, kept.
  • Ceiling: reserve k gas per live byte on forward (max(gas/64, k·mem_bytes)). Gas becomes a composable, forwarded, non-griefable memory budget. No global counter, no new OOM-by-caller.
  • Pick k for policy: k=1 → 16 MiB (below today, more usable per-frame memory); k≈6 → ~2.7 MiB (pins the ceiling at today's single-frame limit).
  • Before shipping: quantify the gas-to-child blast radius on mainnet; that is the one real backward-compat risk, and it is measurable.

Open questions for the report to resolve

  • Target ceiling: match today's peak (~18 MiB), today's single frame (~2.8 MiB), or a deliberate new number?
  • Is the ~9× cost increase for small buffers (if a pure-price approach were chosen instead) acceptable, or is the reservation approach clearly preferred?
  • Blast-radius measurement: mainnet call sites forwarding >63/64-equivalent gas while holding meaningful memory.
